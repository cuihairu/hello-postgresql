# Streaming and Synchronous Replication

Streaming replication (physical replication) streams the WAL records generated by the primary to the standby nearly in real time, and the standby replays the same physical changes to produce a byte-identical copy. Streaming was introduced in 9.0 and synchronous replication in 9.1. This page records the process division of labor on the transport chain, the strength spectrum of `synchronous_commit`, the degradation risk of half-sync, and conflict handling when the standby is readable.

## Processes and the Transport Chain

- Primary side: each streaming replication connection corresponds to one **walsender** process, which reads records from the WAL buffers/files and sends them to the peer.
- Standby side: the **walreceiver** process actively connects to the primary (the connection string is in `primary_conninfo`, usually carried by the config generated by `pg_basebackup`); the WAL it receives is first written to the local `pg_wal`, and the **startup** process then replays it.
- walreceiver reports receive/write/replay progress upstream at `wal_receiver_status_interval` (default 10s), which is the data source for the lag view on the primary side.
- A standby can also cascade: lower-tier standbys connect to the upper-tier standby, reducing the primary's fan-out pressure.

For setup steps (including `pg_basebackup` and `primary_conninfo` configuration), see [Streaming Replication Setup](../operations/streaming-replication-setup).

## The Strength Spectrum of synchronous_commit

`synchronous_commit` decides "before commit returns to the client, how far the WAL must have gotten". It is a session-level parameter and can be set differently per workload:

| Value | Local WAL | Standby received and written to OS | Standby fsync | Standby replayed (queryable) | Data-loss risk on power failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `off` | async | no wait | no wait | no wait | a primary crash may lose the most recent transactions |
| `local` | returns after fsync | no wait | no wait | no wait | the primary is safe, the standby may lag and lose |
| `remote_write` | wait | wait | no wait | no wait | a standby machine power loss may lose data |
| `on` (default) | wait | wait | wait | no wait | standard synchronous replication semantics |
| `remote_apply` | wait | wait | wait | wait | read-your-writes, the largest latency |

`on` guarantees that committed data is on the disk of at least one standby; `remote_apply` further guarantees it is **already queryable** on the standby — use it to implement read-write splitting with "write then immediately read on the standby".

## synchronous_standby_names and Sync Candidates

`synchronous_standby_names` specifies which standbys participate in synchronous acknowledgment, matching by the standby's `application_name` (from its `primary_conninfo`):

```sql
-- Priority mode: the first available one acts as the sync standby, the second is a candidate
synchronous_standby_names = 'FIRST 1 (standby_a, standby_b)';

-- Quorum mode: acknowledgment from any 1 of the two standbys commits
synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby_a, standby_b)';
```

Each standby has a row in the primary's `pg_stat_replication`, and `sync_state` shows its role under the current policy: `sync` (currently acknowledging) / `quorum` / `potential` (candidate) / `async`.

## The Half-Sync Degradation Risk

This is the most important operational discipline for streaming replication: **when `synchronous_commit = on/remote_apply`, if no eligible synchronous standby exists, the primary's write transaction commit blocks and waits indefinitely** — PostgreSQL has no built-in timeout and won't automatically fall back to async. A downed standby means the primary "can't write", until the standby returns or an administrator intervenes:

```sql
-- Emergency: clear the sync list and reload (no restart)
ALTER SYSTEM SET synchronous_standby_names = '';
SELECT pg_reload_conf();
-- Or bypass for just the current session: SET synchronous_commit = 'local';
```

The preventive posture in production: configure at least two sync candidates (`FIRST 1 (a, b)` or `ANY 1 (a, b)`), and alert when `sync_state` is not `sync/quorum` for an extended period. Transactions already committed during the wait are safe; the only risk is that new commits are queued.

## Observing Replay Lag

On the primary side, look at each walsender's progress and timing:

```sql
SELECT application_name, sync_state,
       pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), replay_lsn) AS replay_lag_bytes,
       write_lag, flush_lag, replay_lag
FROM pg_stat_replication;
```

Note that `write_lag/flush_lag/replay_lag` (PG 10+) only refresh when there is new traffic; after the instance goes idle they may sit at stale values or be NULL, so judge "how much lag right now" by the byte difference between `replay_lsn` and `pg_current_wal_lsn()`.

On the standby side, self-check:

```sql
SELECT pg_is_in_recovery() AS in_recovery,
       pg_last_wal_receive_lsn() AS received,
       pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()  AS replayed,
       pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_last_wal_receive_lsn(), pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()) AS not_yet_replayed,
       now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp() AS replay_delay;
```

## Hot Standby Queries and Conflicts

With `hot_standby = on` the standby accepts read-only queries, but replay and queries share the same data: rows vacuumed away on the primary may still be read on the standby by queries holding an old snapshot. At that point replay has two choices — wait for the query, or cancel it:

- `max_standby_streaming_delay` (default 30s): replay yields to a query for at most this long, after which it cancels the conflicting standby query and the client gets `canceling statement due to conflict with recovery`. Setting it to `-1` means wait forever (replay lag grows unboundedly).
- `hot_standby_feedback = on`: the standby reports the minimum transaction snapshot back to the primary, so the primary's VACUUM won't clean up rows the standby is still using — conflicts drop sharply, at the cost of dead tuple buildup (bloat) on the primary; databases with frequent large queries need to weigh this.
- Long replay waits can also trigger lock-type conflicts (e.g. the primary taking `AccessExclusiveLock` on `DROP TABLE`). Standby-side statistics bucketed by type are in `pg_stat_database_conflicts`.

## Notes and Common Pitfalls

- The "safety" of synchronous replication presupposes correct standby configuration: if a standby has `synchronous_commit=off` it only affects its behavior when it is itself a primary; the acknowledgment semantics depend on whether the acknowledging side fsyncs — don't copy the parameter in the wrong direction.
- `remote_apply` has noticeably higher transaction latency; enable it only for sessions that truly need read-your-writes, not database-wide.
- Replay lag alerts should use byte differences (`pg_wal_lsn_diff`) rather than time fields, for the reason about the lag fields' refresh semantics above.
- The rewiring after a primary/standby switchover, and the relationship between sync candidates and HA orchestration, is in [High Availability Architecture](../operations/high-availability-architecture).
